\begin{abstract}
Large language models inherit not only the knowledge but the temporal texture of their training data. Because web-scale corpora carry no uniform date weighting, a model trained on a decade of internet text may treat decade-old slang as equivalent to current usage. We ask whether LLMs track the temporal lifecycle of informal vocabulary or whether their slang usage is frozen relative to the pace of real-world linguistic change. Combining a sense-disambiguated frequency analysis of thirteen slang terms in FineWeb---a dated, filtered derivative of Common Crawl---with three complementary probes of four contemporary LLMs, we find that (i)~model slang usage is anchored to older, well-attested terms irrespective of recency; (ii)~explicitly stating a year in the prompt does not move models toward era-appropriate vocabulary; and (iii)~models' declarative knowledge of when a term was popular is directionally correct but systematically shifted years into the past. We argue these effects compound as human-authored text is increasingly displaced by model output, and discuss implications for training-data curation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation: Training Data Growth and Its Limits}

Large language models have scaled rapidly on ever-growing internet text corpora, and data scale remains a primary lever on capability: compute-optimal training scales tokens in proportion to parameters \citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}. Historically the volume of available training data has grown year over year, making recency a secondary concern. Two trends are shifting this picture. First, LLM-generated text constitutes a growing share of the web, threatening to contaminate future training pools with model output rather than authentic human language; \citet{shumailov2023curse} show that training on model-generated content induces irreversible \textit{model collapse}, in which the tails of the original distribution vanish. Second, if the supply of genuinely human-authored data decelerates, the median age of training data will rise---eventually the bulk of available text may be years or decades old. Both trends point to the same risk: models that anchor their vocabulary to an earlier linguistic era and fail to track how language actually evolves.

\subsection{The Temporal Language Problem}

Language changes continuously, and slang and informal vocabulary change fastest, often rising and falling within months to a few years. Because internet training corpora carry no uniform date weighting, a model trained on a decade of web text may treat decade-old slang as interchangeable with contemporary usage. The problem is most acute for slang of the $\sim$2010--2020 era: it is abundant in training data yet not clearly marked as dated, so its representation is governed by total corpus frequency rather than current relevance (Section~\ref{sec:exp1}). This is compounded by misalignment in the underlying corpora: \citet{cheng2024dated} show that effective training cutoffs often differ substantially from reported ones, and that Common Crawl dumps exhibit non-trivial temporal leakage, with old data persisting in newer dumps and being de facto upsampled.

In this paper we find that \textbf{LLM slang usage is temporally flat}: models do not differentiate by era when producing informal language, leaving their output sounding dated. We expect this effect to worsen as the bulk of high-quality, human-authored pretraining data continues to age.

\subsection{Linguistic Framing}

To demonstrate a temporal linguistic effect we require a set of words whose popularity has measurably risen and fallen within the span of LLM training data. We focus on \textit{slang}, which we situate against two adjacent classes of lexical change:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Neologisms:} words that rise in use and plateau as they enter the standard lexicon. Our hypothesis predicts that models should also lag in adopting neologisms, but their slower, monotonic trajectories are harder to resolve on the time scale of available data. \todo{Add a citation defining neologisms.}

  \item \textbf{``Meme words'' / ephemeral slang:} words that spike quickly and fade within months to a few years---the primary focus of this study. Slang use is both creative and ephemeral \citep{eble1989slang, wu2025slang}. By selecting words that peaked in 2010--2020 and declined before our models' reported cutoffs, we can test whether reduced recent representation in training data translates into reduced model usage.

  \item \textbf{``Vogue words'':} standard-English words that undergo a transient usage spike (e.g.\ \textit{awesome}, \textit{iconic}, \textit{epic}); temporally they behave much like neologisms. \todo{Add a citation defining vogue words.}
\end{itemize}

In linguistics, slang is defined by register rather than novelty, and the definition is famously contested \citep{lighter1978slang, chapman2010slang}. The property we rely on is \textit{ephemerality}: throughout this paper, ``slang'' denotes a word with a demonstrably ephemeral usage lifecycle in the sense of \citet{eble1989slang}, whose definition also ties slang to in-group identity \citep{eble1989slang, earl1972}. We further note that a substantial portion of American internet slang is appropriated African American Vernacular English; we return to this in our discussion of limitations (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).


\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A sense-disambiguated, time-resolved frequency analysis of thirteen slang terms in FineWeb, establishing an empirical ground truth for slang prevalence in LLM training data (Experiment~1).
  \item A semantic-slot probe showing that models reliably produce older, well-attested slang but fail on the most recent terms, and that conditioning the prompt on an explicit year does not recover era-appropriate vocabulary (Experiment~2).
  \item A direct year-association probe showing that models' declarative knowledge of slang timing is directionally correct but systematically shifted into the past, dissociating what models \textit{know} about slang age from what they \textit{use} (Experiment~3).
  \item A discussion of mitigations, including recency-weighted fine-tuning, framed against the compounding risk of model-generated text in future corpora.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background \& Related Work}

\subsection{Training Data Sources and Temporal Coverage}

Common Crawl is a major component of most large-scale training corpora and a rich source of informal language: it includes web text from platforms such as Reddit, whose posts and comments are heavily colloquial, in contrast to sources like BookCorpus that are dominated by edited, formal prose. Owing to Common Crawl's size and noise, a number of cleaned derivatives are distributed on HuggingFace using aggressive deduplication and quality filtering. We build on one such derivative, FineWeb \citep{penedo2024the}, which provides high-quality internet text alongside per-document provenance.

Common Crawl dumps date back to 2013 and have been collected every few months since \citep{commoncrawl2007}; FineWeb retains a \texttt{dump} field linking each document to its originating crawl. The repository releases pre-sampled subsets at several scales, and our analysis uses the 10-billion-token (10BT) sample as a representative slice. This sample was generated in 2024 and therefore spans dumps up to \texttt{CC-MAIN-2024-10} (the tenth week of 2024). The \texttt{dump} field lets us bin documents by date and measure how the frequency of each target slang term varies over time---a corpus-level baseline for slang prevalence on the web, and a means of verifying that each target word is genuinely ephemeral (frequent in one date range and scarce in a later one).

\subsection{Related Work on Emergent Behavior in Large Models}

Larger models exhibit emergent capabilities absent in smaller ones \citep{wei2022emergent}, motivating our use of recent, capable models rather than small ones alone: results from small models may not generalize, and any meta-level awareness of slang recency is both more likely to be present and more likely to surface in generated vocabulary at scale.

\subsection{Related Work on Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Bias}

A growing body of work investigates how training data temporality affects LLM behavior.

\citet{cheng2024dated} find that effective cutoffs often differ from reported cutoffs due to temporal biases of CommonCrawl data and deduplication complications.

\citet{llmlagbench2025} introduce a freshness benchmark for systematically identifying the earliest probable temporal boundaries of an LLM's training data. 

\citet{outdated2024} find that models' understanding of world events effectively lingers around 2015 even for models with nominally later cutoffs --- directly consistent with the temporal freezing hypothesis of this paper.

These works consider cutoff as a primary contributing factor to temporal bias in models, whereas this paper considers amount of data from a given time slice as a contributing factor to model outdatedness.

\subsection{Slang Processing in LLMs}

While LLM capabilities for informal language have received growing attention, the temporal dimension of slang usage has not been systematically studied.

\citet{sun2024slang} benchmark LLM slang knowledge using movie subtitles, covering detection and identification of regional and historical sources of slang. They show GPT-4 performs well as zero-shot but smaller fine-tuned models are competitive.

\citet{wu2025slang} compare human-attested slang from the Online Slang Dictionary against GPT-4o and Llama-3 coined slang, finding significant biases in how LLMs perceive slang and that model slang generation does not align with humans.

Neither of these works examines the \textit{temporal distribution} of slang usage --- i.e., whether models favor older vs.\ newer slang --- which is the central contribution of this paper.

\subsection{Model Collapse and LLM-Generated Data}

The risk of LLM-generated text contaminating future training corpora is increasingly well-documented. \citet{shumailov2023curse} show that training on model-generated content causes irreversible defects where
tails of the original distribution disappear. \citet{drayson2025mgtext}
investigate model collapse under realistic conditions where data origin is unknown, and propose importance sampling as a mitigation.
\citet{feng2024collapse} argue that verification of synthesized data can prevent model collapse.

This body of work motivates our central concern: if model outputs are temporally frozen and increasingly populate the web, future corpora will contain an ever-growing proportion of linguistically dated text. Even where collapse is averted---e.g.\ by verifying synthesized data \citep{feng2024collapse}---the supply of fresh human-authored text may shrink, leaving older data to dominate the high-quality fraction of training corpora. Either path biases future models toward the language of the past.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset: FineWeb}

Our corpus ground truth is drawn from \textbf{FineWeb} \citep{penedo2024the}, a large, quality-filtered, deduplicated text dataset derived from Common Crawl and widely used to train open LLMs. We use the public 10BT sample on HuggingFace, whose documents retain a \texttt{dump} field identifying the originating CC-MAIN crawl (year-week); mapping each crawl to a calendar date lets us slice the sample into a time series spanning roughly 2014--2024.

Working from FineWeb rather than raw Common Crawl WET files has two advantages. First, it already applies the quality and boilerplate filtering characteristic of modern training corpora, so our frequency estimates better reflect the text models actually train on than a raw-web census would. Second, the pre-extracted sample is dramatically cheaper to process than re-crawling and parsing web archives. The trade-off is that FineWeb's own filtering and deduplication may distort raw frequencies, and de-facto temporal misalignment in Common Crawl persists into derivatives \citep{cheng2024dated}; we therefore treat the resulting rates as a representative proxy for slang prevalence in training data rather than an exact measurement of web usage.

\subsection{Identifying Target Slang Words}

We need a reference set of slang words with known temporal peaks. We draw candidates from Word-of-the-Year lists, Urban Dictionary, and Google Search Trends\footnote{Words of the Year tend not to be slang per se --- they often summarize cultural events rather than track informal register --- so they serve only as candidate seeds.}, then keep words that show a clear popularity arc within the analysis window. The final list is \textbf{manually curated from Urban Dictionary} and \textbf{validated against the FineWeb corpus-frequency analysis of Section~\ref{sec:exp1}} so that each word demonstrably exhibits the \textit{ephemeral} property (a clear rise and/or fall over the 2014--2024 window) and fills a recurring informal semantic slot (e.g.\ ``impressive/cool''), keeping meaning roughly controlled across eras. The thirteen words used in the probing experiments group into three temporal bands, mirroring the corpus-frequency figures (Figures~\ref{fig:cc-pre2018}--\ref{fig:cc-2022-24}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pre-2018 (peaked early, now declining):} \textit{lol, sick, troll, bro, swag}.
  \item \textbf{Around 2020 (rising through the late 2010s):} \textit{vibe, vibes, alpha, red pill}.
  \item \textbf{2022--2024 (most recent, sharply rising):} \textit{slay, gaslight, glow-up, aura}.
\end{itemize}

Several of these words are polysemous (e.g.\ \textit{sick}, \textit{troll}, \textit{aura}), with a slang sense distinct from a standard-English sense; Section~\ref{sec:exp1} describes the sense-disambiguation step used to count only the slang usage. This banding lets us compare model behavior on slang that predates the models' training windows against slang that rose near or after the bulk of their training data was collected.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Corpus Frequency Analysis (FineWeb)}
\label{sec:exp1-method}

\textbf{Goal.} Establish the actual temporal frequency distribution of target slang words across time-sliced web data, serving as an empirical ground truth against which LLM output distributions can be compared.

\textbf{Data source.} Rather than re-crawling raw Common Crawl WET files, we use the \textbf{FineWeb} \citep{penedo2024the} 10BT sample hosted on HuggingFace, which carries a \texttt{dump} field tagging each document with its originating CC-MAIN crawl (year-week), allowing us to slice the sample by crawl date. FineWeb is itself a filtered, deduplicated derivative of Common Crawl, so it both reflects the kind of corpus used to train modern LLMs and removes much of the boilerplate that confounds raw-WET counting. Each crawl id maps to a calendar date, giving a time series spanning roughly 2014--2024.

\textbf{Sense disambiguation.} A central difficulty is that many target words are polysemous: \textit{sick} (ill vs.\ excellent), \textit{troll} (creature vs.\ online provocateur), \textit{aura} (energy field vs.\ Gen-Z coolness). Naively counting surface forms conflates the slang sense with unrelated standard usage. To address this we extract the textual context of every target-word occurrence and score it with a \textbf{RoBERTa classifier fine-tuned to distinguish the slang sense} from the standard sense. Only occurrences scoring above a threshold (the figures use $\geq 0.99$) are counted as slang usage.

\textbf{Normalization.} Sense-filtered counts are converted to a rate expressed as occurrences per million tokens of the corresponding dump:
\[
r_{w,d} = \frac{\text{count}_{\text{slang}}(w,\, d)}{\text{tokens}(d)} \times 10^{6}
\]
where \textit{w} is a target word and \textit{d} is a crawl dump. Per-dump token totals are taken from the FineWeb sample metadata. This normalization controls for the fact that successive dumps vary substantially in size.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} The 10BT sample is a random draw from FineWeb, so a per-dump occurrence count $k$ is modeled as $k \sim \text{Poisson}(r_{w,d}\cdot \text{tokens}(d))$. We report the exact Poisson interval (95\% by default) as a shaded band around each word's rate curve; bands are wide for rare words and tight for common ones.

\textbf{Output.} For each target word we plot $r_{w,d}$ against dump date on a $\log_2$ scale, producing the temporal frequency curves in Section~\ref{sec:exp1}. These curves are the corpus ground truth against which the LLM probing distributions are compared.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Semantic Slot Probing}
\label{sec:exp2-method}

\textbf{Goal:} Measure what slang a model actually \textit{produces} when asked for an informal word, with no temporal cue in the prompt, so we observe natural usage rather than meta-knowledge about eras. A model whose vocabulary tracked current usage should readily produce recent slang; a temporally frozen model should fall back on older, well-attested terms. A central design constraint is that we avoid naming years or historical slang in the prompt, which would test meta-knowledge instead of usage.

We give the model a temporally neutral semantic description of a meaning and ask for a single informal word. The probe runs in two modes:

\paragraph{Concept-slot mode.} The description names a general meaning with several era-spanning valid answers (``something impressive/cool'', ``mediocre'', ``charisma'', ``suspicious'', \dots) and we record which word the model reaches for, surfacing its default vocabulary per slot without naming any target word.

\paragraph{Target-recovery mode.} The description is written to point at one specific target word from Section~\ref{sec:exp1-method} --- e.g.\ a definition that uniquely evokes \textit{gaslight} or \textit{aura} --- and we measure how often the model returns that exact word, counting stem and inflection matches (so \textit{glow-up}/\textit{glowup} and \textit{gaslight}/\textit{gaslighting} all count). Recovery rate is a direct measure of how reluctant the model is to produce the intended term, and of whether the term is effectively out of vocabulary. Each target word is probed with three independently worded phrasings to control for wording, and each phrasing is sampled ten times per model.

\paragraph{Year-conditioned variant.} To test whether a model conditions its vocabulary on a stated time, we prepend ``The year is \{year\}.'' to each target-recovery prompt and sweep \{year\} over 2013--2025. If a model were aware of its own data distribution --- i.e.\ knew which slang belongs to which year --- recovery accuracy for a word should be highest at, or near, the year of that word's true corpus peak (Experiment~1). Flat accuracy across years would indicate the model ignores the temporal cue; a peak at the wrong year would indicate a miscalibrated sense of timing.

We probe four models spanning three providers: two open-weight models served via Together AI --- \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo} and \texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro} --- and two closed models, \texttt{gpt-4o} (OpenAI) and \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6} (Anthropic). Because the closed APIs do not expose token logprobs, responses are sampled (temperature~1.0) and summarized over samples by frequency. Notably, the Llama model includes RLHF, which as we see in the results may suppress slang and act as a confounding factor.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Direct Year Association}
\label{sec:exp3-method}

\textbf{Goal:} Experiment~2 measures \textit{usage} --- what slang the model reaches for when it is not told to think about time. Experiment~3 instead measures \textit{meta-knowledge}: when asked directly, does the model know \textit{when} a slang word was popular? Contrasting the two isolates whether any temporal failure is a knowledge gap or a usage bias. This is the inverse of the Experiment~2 design constraint: here we deliberately make the temporal dimension explicit.

We use three prompt forms, each rendered once per target word and sampled across all four models:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GiveYear:} \textit{``The slang word `\{word\}' is most associated with what year? Respond using only the year.''} --- a direct request for the word's peak year.
  \item \textbf{DatePhrase:} \textit{``You hear someone saying `\{word\}' in conversation, which year are they most likely saying this in?''} --- a softer, scenario-framed variant of the same question.
  \item \textbf{AssociateSlangLogprobs:} an echo+logprob probe over open models that scores the joint completion logprob of each \textit{(year, word)} pairing, giving a continuous association surface rather than a single point estimate.
\end{itemize}

GiveYear and DatePhrase are sampled five times per word per model, and AssociateSlangLogprobs is restricted to the open-weight models; we parse a four-digit year from each free-text response. Comparing the model's stated peak year against the corpus peak from Experiment~1 tells us whether a model's \textit{declarative} knowledge of slang timing is accurate even where its \textit{generative} usage is temporally flat.

\subsection{Model Selection}

All probes are run on the four instruction-tuned models introduced in Section~\ref{sec:exp2-method}, spanning three providers and a mix of open- and closed-weight systems; the open models additionally expose token logprobs, letting us cross-check sampled-frequency estimates against direct probabilities. We deliberately span target words from the pre-2018 band through 2022--2024 so that, for every model, some target slang predates the bulk of its training data while other terms rose near or after its effective cutoff. This separation distinguishes a genuine recency bias---older slang overrepresented relative to current usage---from the trivial case of recent slang being absent from training data altogether.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Semantic Slot Probing}
\label{sec:exp2}

We first report aggregate target-word recovery --- how often each intended slang term is produced from its definition --- then the year-conditioned variant, which tests whether stating a year shifts recovery toward the word's true era.

\subsubsection{Target-word recovery}

\textbf{Target-recovery accuracy varies sharply by word, not just by model.} Figure~\ref{fig:slotaccuracy} shows, for each target word, the percentage of responses that recovered the intended word, one bar per model (ten samples per phrasing, three phrasings per word). Aggregate recovery is highest for \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6} (71\%), followed by \texttt{gpt-4o} (62\%) and \texttt{DeepSeek-V4-Pro} (60\%), with \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B} lowest at 53\% --- consistent with RLHF-driven slang suppression in the Llama model. Unambiguous, well-established terms (\textit{lol}, \textit{troll}, \textit{gaslight}, \textit{glow-up}) are recovered near-perfectly by every model, whereas polysemous or contested slots are hard across the board: \textit{sick} (models answer the literal ``awesome''/``cool'' rather than the slang \textit{sick}), \textit{swag} (models prefer the competing \textit{swagger}/\textit{drip}), and the most recent term \textit{aura} (models reach for \textit{clout}, \textit{charisma}, or \textit{vibe}) are recovered well below a third of the time for most models.

\textbf{The hardest slots are the most diagnostic.} That \textit{aura} --- the newest term, still sharply rising in the corpus (Figure~\ref{fig:cc-2022-24}) --- is the word models are least able to produce on demand is consistent with the central hypothesis: the slang that is most current in human usage is the slang the models are least fluent in. Conversely, the words recovered most reliably are the older, well-attested terms that have saturated the training data.

\subsubsection{Conditioning on a stated year}

The year-conditioned variant prepends ``The year is \{year\}.'' to each recovery prompt and sweeps the year over 2013--2025. If a model tracked its own data distribution, recovery for a word should be highest at that word's true corpus peak year. Figure~\ref{fig:slotyear} shows the result as a word\,$\times$\,prompt-year accuracy heatmap for \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, with rows ordered by true corpus peak and each word's peak year outlined.

\textbf{The stated year has little effect.} For the majority of words---\textit{troll}, \textit{bro}, \textit{lol}, \textit{gaslight}, \textit{vibe}, \textit{vibes}---accuracy is essentially flat (near 100\%) across all thirteen prompt years: changing ``2013'' to ``2024'' leaves the output unchanged. The model is not conditioning its vocabulary on the stated time.

\textbf{Where accuracy does vary, it does not track the true peak --- and sometimes anti-tracks it.} Among the words with genuine year-to-year variation, the high-accuracy cells do not line up with the outlined true-peak cell. The starkest case is \texttt{gpt-4o}'s \textit{red pill} (true corpus peak 2024): recovery is \emph{highest} in the earliest prompt years ($\sim$80\% at 2013) and \emph{lowest} at the true peak (23\% at 2024). Claude's \textit{alpha} (peak 2020) is recovered at 100\% through 2013--2017 but dips to $\sim$67\% around its actual peak. If anything, models recover a word slightly better when told an \emph{earlier} year --- the same backward-shifted sense of timing quantified in Experiment~3.

\begin{figure}

\subsection{Experiment 3: Direct Year Association}
\label{sec:exp3}

Experiment~2 measures what slang the models \textit{use}. Experiment~3 instead asks what the models \textit{know} about slang timing, by posing the temporal question directly (Section~\ref{sec:exp3-method}). The contrast is the key result: the models carry \emph{directionally} correct declarative knowledge of when slang was popular --- they order terms by era far better than chance --- yet that knowledge is both noticeably shifted toward the past and not reflected in their spontaneous usage (Experiment~2).

\textbf{The figure overlays model belief against corpus ground truth.} Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc} places the target words on the y-axis \emph{ordered by their true corpus peak year} (from Experiment~1), with calendar year on the x-axis. For each word a red star marks the corpus peak and a dark diamond marks the model's mean responded year, pooling the \textit{GiveYear} and \textit{DatePhrase} samples; the line connecting them is the model's error, and the faint points show the spread of individual responses. Because the words are sorted by true peak, the stars form a rising staircase, so the horizontal offset of each diamond from its star reads directly as how far the model is off.

\textbf{Models order slang roughly by era but compress it toward the past.} The diamonds broadly follow the staircase --- models do place \textit{lol}, \textit{troll}, and \textit{bro} early and \textit{aura} and \textit{slay} late --- but they systematically date the newer terms too early. For \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, \textit{alpha} (corpus peak 2020) is placed near 2010, and \textit{vibe}/\textit{vibes} (still rising through 2024) near 2018/2020. The mean absolute error against the corpus peak is sizeable for every model: 6.2 years for Claude, 7.7 for Llama, and 8.0 for DeepSeek, consistent with a sense of slang anchored to an earlier window than current usage.

\textbf{Caveat on the comparison.} The corpus ``peak'' is the year of maximum frequency, so for the words still rising at the end of the data window (2023--2024 peaks) it is closer to a lower bound on recency than a true crest; a model dating \textit{vibe} to 2018 is arguably reporting the \emph{onset} of popular use rather than the corpus peak. The comparison is cleanest for words that have visibly crested within the window (\textit{lol}, \textit{swag}, \textit{alpha}), where the models still skew early. \texttt{gpt-4o} is a special case: its lower 4.4-year error is an artifact rather than better calibration, since its \textit{DatePhrase} responses collapse to ``2023'' (the apparent present) for almost every word, which happens to sit near the many recent peaks.

The models possess \emph{directional} temporal knowledge --- they order slang by era far better than chance --- but that knowledge is both compressed toward the past and, critically, not reflected in their actual usage (Experiment~2). The failure to produce current slang is therefore not a total absence of temporal knowledge, but a usage bias compounded by a backward-shifted sense of when ``recent'' slang belongs.

\section{Results}

Findings from the corpus analysis and the probing experiments converge on a consistent pattern:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The target words have genuine, separable lifecycles.} The corpus analysis (Experiment~1, Figures~\ref{fig:cc-pre2018}--\ref{fig:cc-2022-24}) confirms that the older terms (\textit{lol}, \textit{swag}) are in decline by the end of the window while the newest terms (\textit{aura}, \textit{slay}, \textit{glow-up}) are still rising. This is what makes the words usable as a temporal probe and validates the curated target set.

  \item \textbf{Models are least fluent in the most current slang.} In semantic-slot recovery (Experiment~2, Figure~\ref{fig:slotaccuracy}), every model recovers older, unambiguous terms (\textit{lol}, \textit{troll}, \textit{gaslight}, \textit{glow-up}) near-perfectly, but recovery collapses for the newest term \textit{aura} and for polysemous slots (\textit{sick}, \textit{swag}) where the model prefers a competing or literal word. The slang that is most current in human usage is the slang the models are least able to produce on demand.

  \item \textbf{An explicit year cue does not fix it.} Prefixing the recovery prompt with ``The year is \{year\}.'' (Experiment~2, Figure~\ref{fig:slotyear}) barely changes which slang the model produces: most words are recovered at a flat rate across 2013--2025, and the words that do vary do not peak at their true corpus-peak year --- for the most recent terms (\textit{red pill}, \textit{vibe}) accuracy is, if anything, highest when an \emph{earlier} year is named. Models do not use a stated date to retrieve era-appropriate vocabulary.

  \item \textbf{Knowing-when and using-now come apart.} The year-association probes (Experiment~3, Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc}) show that models order slang by era directionally correctly, but place the newer terms years too early relative to the corpus peak (mean absolute error 6--8 years per model), and their spontaneous usage does not track recency at all. The temporal failure is thus a usage bias on top of a backward-shifted, rather than absent, sense of slang timing.

  \item \textbf{Model-level differences are consistent with RLHF slang suppression.} \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B} has the lowest semantic-slot recovery (53\% vs.\ 71\% for Claude) and most readily defaults to neutral standard-English words, consistent with reports that RLHF biases toward Standard American English \citep{barnhart2025rlhf}. \texttt{gpt-4o} exhibits a distinct artifact --- collapsing \textit{DatePhrase} answers to the present year --- that is orthogonal to the era bias but worth noting. \todo{Confirm whether DeepSeek/Claude RLHF pipelines differ enough to explain the recovery-rate spread.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}

Results suggest the models do not display temporal sensitivity in their spontaneous slang usage --- when asked to produce current slang on demand they reliably return older, well-attested terms and struggle most with the newest ones (Experiment~2), and an explicit year cue does not move them toward era-appropriate vocabulary --- even while they retain \emph{directionally} correct declarative knowledge of when those terms were popular (Experiment~3). This is consistent with the hypothesis that model slang \textit{usage} reflects total frequency in the training corpus rather than a temporally calibrated view of what is current. \todo{Strengthen the framing with linguistics citations on the social and identity-signaling function of slang, motivating why temporally miscalibrated informal vocabulary matters independently of the downstream training-data argument.}

The difficulty models have producing the most recent term \textit{aura} --- still sharply rising in the corpus at the end of the window --- is striking precisely because the same models date it to roughly the right era (2023--2024) when asked directly (Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc}). \textit{aura} is the cleanest illustration of usage-bias-without-a-knowledge-gap: the model knows when the word belongs yet will not produce it on demand. For most other recent terms the model additionally shifts its stated date years too early, so the two effects --- backward-shifted belief and a usage bias on top of it --- compound. The reliability gap between recovering older versus newer slang may reflect that the older terms have saturated the training data with strong, redundant signal, whereas the newest terms appear in quantities too small (and too close to the data-collection horizon) to anchor confident generation, despite being the dominant form in current human usage.

\subsection{Implications for Training Data}

If models cannot keep pace with linguistic change, the effect compounds as web text becomes increasingly model-generated \citep{shumailov2023curse}: output reflecting frozen, older vocabulary re-enters future training corpora, further entrenching dated language and eroding models' capacity to represent current human usage. This connects to the ``stochastic parrots'' critique \citep{bender2021parrots}---models capture the statistical structure of language but need not track its dynamics.

The practical effect today is modest, and is partly masked by instruction-tuned models' general reluctance to produce slang at all (Section~\ref{sec:exp2}). The longer-term concern is both larger and more general than slang: the same mechanism applies to any generational language shift. As models are retrained on the accumulated text of past decades---increasingly including their own outputs---the language they produce may drift steadily further from contemporary usage. Mitigating this will require explicit training interventions, such as recency-weighted sampling, together with a sustained supply of fresh, verifiably human-authored text. Our results thus add weight to the case for treating human-generated text as a premium training resource.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Prompt register as a confound.} Our probes ask for slang in a neutral register. A user who prompts in contemporary, slang-laden English might condition the model toward more current vocabulary, in which case the practical impact of the temporal drift we measure would be smaller than the probes suggest. We do not vary prompt register and cannot rule this out. \todo{Add an experiment that systematically varies prompt register (formal vs.\ contemporary-casual) to measure how much input language conditions the recency of output slang.}

\paragraph{Further limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Cross-model comparisons confound knowledge cutoff with architecture, training data, and alignment procedure (e.g.\ RLHF); we therefore emphasize within-model temporal patterns over cross-model magnitudes.
  \item All four probed models are instruction-tuned. RLHF and instruction tuning are known to suppress non-standard English \citep{barnhart2025rlhf} and may amplify the apparent temporal freeze; replicating on base models would isolate this effect.
  \item Much American internet slang is appropriated African American Vernacular English. We do not analyze this dimension, though documented model bias against non-standard English \citep{barnhart2025rlhf} likely interacts with the effects we report.
  \item Quality filtering and deduplication in Common Crawl derivatives can distort raw term frequencies; our FineWeb-based rates inherit any such distortion, and the de-facto upsampling of older dumps \citep{cheng2024dated} biases the corpus baseline toward earlier usage.
  \item The logprob-based probe (\textit{AssociateSlangLogprobs}) runs only on the two open-weight models, since the closed APIs do not expose token logprobs; closed-model distributions are approximated by repeated sampling, which is noisier.
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================
\section{Future Work}

\paragraph{Fine-tuning as mitigation.} The most direct test of our framing is interventional: fine-tune a model on a recency-weighted subset of the data---upsampling recent dumps, or downsampling older ones---and re-run the probes. If the temporal freeze is driven by aggregate corpus frequency, recency weighting should shift recovery toward newer slang, whereas no change would point to a deeper architectural or alignment cause. \todo{Review the literature on recency-weighted and continual-learning training for LLMs before designing this experiment.}

\paragraph{Naturalistic generation.} Our recovery probe forces a single-word answer. A complementary experiment would measure the rate at which target slang appears in free, in-context generation (e.g.\ a casual message or social-media reply), giving an ecologically valid estimate of usage to set against the forced-choice recovery rates. \todo{Run a controlled free-generation probe and report per-era slang production rates with matched register prompts.}

\paragraph{Further directions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Sub-year temporal resolution: can models distinguish slang peaks within a single year (e.g.\ fast-moving meme words)?
  \item Replicating the probes on base (non-instruction-tuned) checkpoints to isolate the contribution of RLHF to slang suppression.
  \item A longitudinal study tracking vocabulary drift as models are retrained on successive crawl snapshots (resource-intensive, as it requires repeated pretraining).
  \item Multilingual analysis: low-resource languages may be affected more strongly (model output can quickly dominate scarce human data) or less (smaller corpora may be more easily refreshed by recent text).
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiment 1: FineWeb Corpus Frequency}
\label{app:exp1}

\textbf{Data.} The public FineWeb \citep{penedo2024the} 10BT sample on HuggingFace, comprising 95 dated Common Crawl (\texttt{CC-MAIN}) dumps spanning 2013--2024. Each document's \texttt{dump} field is mapped to the Monday of its ISO year-week to place it on a calendar axis.

\textbf{Sense disambiguation.} For each target word we extract every occurrence with its surrounding context and score it with a RoBERTa classifier fine-tuned to separate the slang sense from the standard-English sense (e.g.\ \textit{sick} ill vs.\ excellent, \textit{aura} energy-field vs.\ Gen-Z coolness). The paper's figures count only occurrences scoring $\geq 0.99$; the threshold is a tunable parameter.

\textbf{Normalization and uncertainty.} Sense-filtered counts are converted to occurrences per million tokens of the corresponding dump, $r_{w,d} = 10^{6}\cdot \text{count}_{\text{slang}}(w,d) / \text{tokens}(d)$, using per-dump token totals from the FineWeb sample metadata. Each per-dump count is treated as a Poisson draw, and the figures show the exact (Garwood) 95\% Poisson interval as a shaded band.

\textbf{Plotting.} Frequency curves use a $\log_2$ y-axis with a 5-dump centered moving average; dumps with zero sense-filtered occurrences are omitted so the line connects neighboring dumps rather than dropping to the floor. A linear-axis (\texttt{\_count}) version of each figure is also provided. The corpus peak year (Table~\ref{tab:words}) is the argmax, over calendar years, of the year-pooled normalized rate.

\textbf{Implementation.} \texttt{FineWebAnalysis/}: \texttt{roberta\_filter.py} (context scoring), \texttt{word\_rate\_plotter.py} (frequency curves), \texttt{peak\_year.py} (peak-year extraction).

\section{Experiment 2: Semantic Slot Probing}
\label{app:exp2}

All slot prompts share the system message \texttt{"Respond with only a single word or very short phrase -- no explanation, no punctuation."} and are sampled at temperature~1.0.

\textbf{Concept-slot mode.} Six meaning slots, each with four independently worded phrasings; the model's single-word answer is recorded without naming any target. The first phrasing of each slot:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textit{cool/impressive:} ``What's a single informal word someone might use to describe something they think is very impressive or cool?''
  \item \textit{mediocre:} ``What's a single informal word for something that is average, unimpressive, or not worth your time?''
  \item \textit{charisma:} ``What's a single informal word for someone's natural ability to effortlessly attract others or come across as charming?''
  \item \textit{agreement:} ``What's a single informal word someone uses to express strong enthusiastic agreement or approval?''
  \item \textit{suspicious:} ``What's a single informal word for when something about a person or situation feels off, untrustworthy, or not right?''
  \item \textit{excellent (sensory):} ``What's a single informal word someone would use to say a song, food, or piece of art is exceptionally good?''
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiment 3: Direct Year Association}
\label{app:exp3}

These probes share the system message \texttt{"Give the response as a single returned token or word."} Two are free-text and one is a logprob probe:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{GiveYear:} ``The slang word `\{word\}' is most associated with what year? Respond using only the year.''
  \item \textbf{DatePhrase:} ``You hear someone saying `\{word\}' in conversation, which year are they most likely saying this in?''
  \item \textbf{AssociateSlangLogprobs:} ``A standout word in term of popularity incrase in \{year\} was \{word\}'' --- an echo${+}$logprob probe (\texttt{logprobs=5}, \texttt{echo=true}) that scores the joint completion logprob of each \textit{(year, word)} pair over years 2015--2025.
\end{itemize}

\noindent GiveYear and DatePhrase are rendered once per target word and sampled five times per word on all four models; a four-digit year is parsed from each response. AssociateSlangLogprobs runs only on the two open-weight models, since the closed APIs do not expose token logprobs. The mean responded year per word, pooled over GiveYear and DatePhrase, is plotted against the corpus peak in Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc}.
