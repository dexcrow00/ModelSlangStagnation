\begin{abstract}
The accuracy of large language models is an integral aspect of their performance. Accuracy is commonly thought of in terms of grammar and informational correctness, but also importantly encompasses the correct usage of vocabulary by models. We argue that this ``correct usage'' encompasses not just avoiding the use of definitionally incorrect words and phrases, but avoiding the use of irrelevant and dated vocabulary. Large language models inherit not only the knowledge but the temporal vocabulary characteristics of their training data. Because web-scale corpora carry no uniform date weighting, a model trained on a decade of internet text may treat decade-old slang as equivalent to current usage. We ask whether LLMs track the temporal lifecycle of informal vocabulary or whether their slang usage is stunted relative to the pace of real-world linguistic change. Combining a frequency analysis of slang terms in FineWeb~\cite{penedo2024the}---a filtered derivative of Common Crawl---with three complementary analyses of four LLMs, we find that (i)~model slang usage is anchored to older, well-attested terms irrespective of recency. The tested models use slang with average peak usage over ten years ago; (ii)~even with explicit instruction models display inaccurate awareness of peak slang usage; and (iii)~models' parametric knowledge of when a term was popular is systematically dated much earlier than its peak popularity based on the FineWeb corpus. We argue these effects compound as human-authored text is increasingly displaced by model output, and discuss implications for future training-data curation, as well as potential societal impacts from common usage of models that tend to stagnate language. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation: Training Data Growth and Its Limits}
Large language models have scaled rapidly on ever-growing internet text corpora, and data scale remains a primary lever for capability: compute-optimal training scales tokens in proportion to parameters \citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, \citep{zhao2023survey}. Historically internet traffic and the volume of available training data has continued to grow year over year, making recency a secondary concern \citep{villalobos2024datalimits, zhao2023survey, reinsel2018digitization}. Two trends are shifting this picture. First, LLM-generated text constitutes a growing share of the web, threatening to contaminate future training pools with model output rather than authentic human language; \citet{shumailov2023curse} show that training on model-generated content induces irreversible \textit{model collapse}, in which the tails of the original distribution vanish. Second, if the supply of genuinely human-authored data decelerates, the median age of training data will rise until eventually the bulk of available text may be years or decades old. It as been demonstrated that models lean on older data \citep{zhao2024temporal,zhang2023mitigatingtemporal, luu2022temporal, cheng2024dated}, we show that this has effects not only on a model's knowledge of events and information, but on its \textbf{view of language itself}. This fundamental flaw in model design leads models to inherently anchor their vocabulary to an earlier linguistic era and fail to track how language actually evolves.

\subsection{The Temporal Language Problem}

Language changes continuously, with slang and informal vocabulary changing at the fastest rate, often rising and falling within months to a few years. Because internet training corpora carry no uniform date weighting, a model trained on a decade of web text may treat decade-old slang as interchangeable with contemporary usage, due to inherent lack of awareness of the structure of their own training data. The problem is most acute for slang of the $\sim$2010--2020 era: it is abundant in training data yet not clearly dated, so its representation is governed by total corpus frequency rather than current relevance (Section~\ref{sec:exp1}). This is compounded by misalignment in the underlying corpora: \citet{cheng2024dated} show that effective training cutoffs often differ substantially from reported ones, and that Common Crawl dumps exhibit non-trivial temporal leakage, with old data persisting in newer dumps and being de facto upsampled.

In this paper we find that \textbf{LLM slang usage is temporally flat}: models do not differentiate by era when producing informal language, leaving their freeform output sounding dated due to higher representation of older slang in training corpora. We theorize that the "temporal misalignments" shown in \citep{cheng2024dated} also effect the language used by the model itself. We expect this effect to worsen if the bulk of high-quality, human-authored pretraining data continues to age without intervention.

\subsection{Linguistic Framing}

To demonstrate a temporal linguistic effect we require a set of words whose popularity has measurably risen and fallen within the span of LLM training data. We focus on \textit{slang}, which we situate against two adjacent classes of words or phrases defined by lexical change:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Neologisms:} words that rise in use and plateau as they enter the standard lexicon \citep{metcalf2002predicting}. Our hypothesis predicts that models should also lag in adopting neologisms, but their slower, monotonic trajectories are harder to resolve on the time scale of available data.

  \item \textbf{``Meme words'' / ephemeral slang:} words that spike quickly and fade within months to a few years. Due to the current max age (~3 decades) of most colloquial text training data (internet text), this time range is ideal for our study. Slang use is both creative and ephemeral \citep{eble1989slang, wu2025slang}, for our study we select words for their ephemerality. By selecting words that peaked in 2010--2020 and declined before our models' reported cutoffs, we can test whether reduced recent representation in training data translates into reduced usage by models.

  \item \textbf{``Vogue words'':} standard-English words that undergo a transient usage spike (e.g.\ \textit{awesome}, \textit{iconic}, \textit{epic}) coined in \citep{fowler1926dictionary}; temporally they behave much like neologisms. This category is also examined in this study, but should be differentiated from words that increase in usage frequency due to a new definition, which we attempt to separate. That separation is important: lexical change concentrates in new senses attaching to existing forms rather than in newly coined forms \citep{hamilton2016polysemy}, so a frequency spike is only evidence of \textit{slang} adoption once the slang sense has been isolated from the standard one (Section~\ref{sec:exp1-method}).
\end{enumerate}

In linguistics, slang is defined by register rather than novelty, but the definition is famously contested \citep{lighter1978slang, chapman2010slang}. The property we rely on to define slang here is \textit{ephemerality}: throughout this paper, ``slang'' denotes a word with a demonstrably ephemeral usage lifecycle in the sense of \citet{eble1989slang}. Additionally, in order to demonstrate the effects of models using aging slang, we cite examples of slang tied to in-group identity \citep{eble1989slang, earl1972}, in particular in-group to a specific generation (we often contrast "Millenial" and "Gen-Z" slang usage). The authors would also like to acknowledge that a substantial portion of American internet slang is appropriated African American Vernacular English or language appropriated from the Queer community; we return to this in our discussion of limitations (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A sense-disambiguated, time-resolved frequency analysis of nineteen slang terms in FineWeb, establishing a ground truth for slang prevalence in LLM training data and a proxy for the popularity of the terms at given time slices (Experiment~1).
  \item A direct year-association experiment showing that models' knowledge of slang era is systematically shifted into the past, dissociating what models \textit{know} about slang age from how they \textit{use} it (Experiment~2).
  \item A semantic-slot experiment showing that models reliably produce older, well-attested slang but fail on the most recent terms, and that conditioning the prompt on an explicit year does not recover era-appropriate vocabulary (Experiment~3).
  \item An analysis of slang words generated by model persona simulation, mapping them back to corpus age to demonstrate a model "vintage" based on average age of slang used in a naturalistic setting (Experiment~4).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background \& Related Work}

\subsection{Training Data Sources and Temporal Coverage}

Common Crawl is a major component of most large-scale training corpora and a rich source of informal language: it includes web text from platforms such as Reddit\footnote{Reddit closed its API to crawlers in 2024, which is the end date of the FineWeb CommonCrawl basis in this study.}, whose posts and comments capture a large variety of informal language and internet slang, in contrast to sources like BookCorpus that are dominated by edited formal prose. Owing to Common Crawl's size and noise, a number of cleaned derivatives are distributed on HuggingFace using aggressive deduplication and quality filtering. We examine one popular derivative, FineWeb \citep{penedo2024the}, which provides high-quality internet text using a set of novel and established filtering methods (See https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/fineweb\#data-processing-steps).

Common Crawl dumps date back to 2013 and have been collected every few months since \citep{commoncrawl2007}. FineWeb retains a \texttt{dump} field linking each document to its originating crawl. The repository releases pre-sampled subsets at several scales, and our analysis uses the 10-billion-token (10BT) sample as a representative slice. This sample was generated in 2024 and so spans dumps up to \texttt{CC-MAIN-2024-10} (the tenth week of 2024). The \texttt{dump} field lets us bin documents by date, typically to the granularity of a few months, and measure how the frequency of each target slang term varies over time. This is used as a corpus-level baseline for slang prevalence on the web, and a means of verifying that each target word is genuinely ephemeral (frequent in one date range and scarce in a later one). Additionally, this analysis gives us insights into which words have recently become popular and which have recently fallen out of favor.
Common Crawl itself is also the source of many open questions around the representativeness of data used to traing LLMs \citep{baack2024commoncrawl}, \citep{luccioni2021commoncrawl}.

\subsection{Temporal Misalignment}
\label{sec:rw-temporal}

This paper is a direct extension of work on \textit{temporal misalignment}: the mismatch between the period a model's training data was drawn from and the period in which the model is deployed.

\paragraph{Measuring temporal misalignment.} \citet{luu2022temporal} formalize the problem by training and evaluating models on time-sliced splits of several domains, showing that performance degrades as the train--test time gap widens, that the rate of degradation is strongly task-dependent, and that neither model scale nor continued pretraining eliminates it. \citet{agarwal2022temporal} reach a compatible conclusion for pretrained encoders on standard NLP tasks, and further localize the damage: deterioration concentrates on items whose \textit{surface forms} are new to the model, for example vocabulary, rather than on syntax or task structure, which can be partly repaired by continued pretraining on text from the evaluation period.

\paragraph{Where the misalignment originates.} \citet{cheng2024dated} supply the mechanism on the data side, showing that a model's \textit{effective} cutoff frequently precedes its reported one, because Common Crawl dumps leak old documents forward and imperfect deduplication effectively upsamples them. What governs how strongly a period is represented is the volume of text surviving from each slice of time, a central mechanism explored in this paper.

\paragraph{From facts to language.} We apply this framework one level down: to the lexicon a model speaks in rather than the propositions it asserts. This is a change of signal rather than of subject, and each construct above has a lexical counterpart that we measure.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A ground truth indexed by time.} Where \citet{luu2022temporal} use time-sliced task labels, we use the sense-disambiguated corpus-frequency of a slang term across FineWeb dumps (Experiment~1). Slang is what makes this substitution possible: a fact flips from true to false, whereas a slang term traces a measurable lifecycle of rise and decline that allows us to evaluate slang usage's appropriateness (as a proxy for correctness) around a given time period.
  \item \textbf{Facts that expire.} \citet{zhang2023mitigatingtemporal} predict how long a fact stays true; our ephemerality criterion (Section~\ref{sec:targets}) asks the same question of a word, with the duration measured from the corpus. Unlike fact based evaluation, where an outdated fact is \textit{wrong} and can be discarded, outdated slang remains grammatical and licit, so no training or evaluation objective penalizes it. Lexical misalignment is nuanced and missed by accuracy-based evaluations this literature relies on.
  \item \textbf{Resetting the clock.} \citet{zhao2024temporal} show that a year cue in the prompt substantially realigns factual behavior. We run the direct lexical analogue (Experiment~3) and find that it does \textit{not} recover recent vocabulary.Vocabulary appears to be less associated with specific time, as further demonstrated in Experiment~2.
  \item \textbf{Surviving text per slice.} \citet{cheng2024dated} treat corpus temporal leakage as a nuisance that corrupts the reported cutoff. Because a term's representation reflects total accumulated frequency rather than current relevance, a decade of accumulated 2010s text keeps decade-old slang dominant over terms that have only recently begun to rise.
\end{enumerate}

In short, this body of work establishes that models are anchored in the past with respect to \textit{what they know}. We show that they are anchored in the past with respect to \textit{how they talk}, that this second anchoring survives the interventions that repair the first, and that it carries a socio-cultural cost that accuracy-oriented benchmarks do not register.

\paragraph{Related Work on Slang Processing and Understanding in LLMs}

Both \citet{sun2024slang} and \citet{wu2025slang} are recent papers exploring LLM slang usage. Neither of these works examines the \textit{temporal distribution} of slang usage, whether models favor older vs.\ newer slang, which is the central contribution of this paper. LLMs weaknesses in slang understanding and inability to generate slang in a way similar to human's, as demonstrated by the above papers, does provide additional support to our arguments that slang erasure is a real technical problem with models with real societal impact.

\paragraph{Model Collapse and LLM-Generated Data}

The risk of LLM-generated text contaminating future training corpora is increasingly well-documented \citet{shumailov2023curse, rahmani2025synthetic,drayson2025mgtext,feng2024collapse}. \citet{shumailov2023curse} show that training on model-generated content causes irreversible defects where tails of the original distribution disappear.

This body of work motivates our central concern: if model outputs are temporally frozen and increasingly populate the web, future corpora will contain an ever-growing proportion of linguistically dated ``synthetic'' text. Even using detection to avert collapse (such as proposed by \citep{drayson2025mgtext}) the supply of fresh human-authored text is may shrink, leaving older data to dominate the high-quality fraction of training corpora.

\section{Methodology}

\paragraph{Dataset: FineWeb}

Our corpus baseline on slang trends is drawn from \textbf{FineWeb} \citep{penedo2024the}, a large, quality-filtered, deduplicated text dataset derived from Common Crawl and widely used to train open LLMs. We use the public 10BT sample on HuggingFace, whose documents retain a \texttt{dump} field identifying the originating CC-MAIN crawl (year-week). Dumps are available from 2013 to 2024, giving us a wide range to test for word ephemerality taking place on the scale of years or months.

Working from FineWeb rather than raw Common Crawl files has two advantages. First, it already applies the quality and filtering of modern training corpora, so our frequency estimates better reflect the text models actually train on than a raw Common Crawl would. Second, the pre-extracted sample is much cheaper to process than re-crawling and parsing the full web archives. Regarding the use of FineWeb as a proxy for internet usage trends overall, while FineWeb's own filtering and deduplication may distort raw frequencies, CommonCrawl's imperfect de-deduplication processes mean that it is not a perfect representation of real internet language usage either. We therefore treat the resulting rates as a representative proxy for slang prevalence, with high relevance to LLM training, in training data rather than an exact measurement of web usage.

\paragraph{Model Selection}
We run our query based vocabulary experiments on four models: \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo} and \texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro} provided via Together AI, and two closed models, \texttt{gpt-4o} (OpenAI) and \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6} (Anthropic). Larger models show emergent capabilities that smaller ones lack \citep{wei2022emergent}. This is why we experiment on recent, capable models rather than small ones alone: small-model results may not carry over, and whatever awareness of slang recency a model has is both more likely to exist and more likely to surface in its output at scale.

We test four models spanning three providers: two open-weight models served via Together AI, \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo} and \texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro}, and two closed models, \texttt{gpt-4o} (OpenAI) and \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6} (Anthropic). Because the closed APIs do not expose token logprobs, responses are sampled (temperature=1.0) and summarized over samples by frequency.

\paragraph{Identifying Target Slang Words}
\label{sec:targets}

The main analysis is divided into two main methodologies, based on the source of the set of slang words in an experiment. The initial experiments involve identifying a reference set of slang words in the FineWeb corpus, and subsequently running analysis on their frequencies across FineWeb dumps to profile their temporal characteristics. We then examine how models use and view this set of corpus identified slang words. The second set of experiments starts by prompting a set of models with persona-style prompts intended to generate free-form slang usage. We use the responses to generate a list of slang commonly used by models, which we can then run analysis against in the FineWeb corpus. This gives us chracteristics of the slang words most commonly used by the set of models, such as peak usage age in the corpus for a given word. 
For the set of slang words used for initial analysis in FineWeb, we draw 125 candidates\footnote{144 total words are show in the appendix analysis. The additional 19 words come from Experiment 3, where we collect additional words of interest from the models directly.} from Word-of-the-Year lists, Urban Dictionary, and Google Search Trends\footnote{Words of the Year tend not to be slang per se, but instead often relate to global events. Using Urban Dictionary and cross referencing word popularity using Google Search Trends was the most reliable way to find good candidate words.}. Each candidate is then \textbf{validated against the FineWeb corpus-frequency analysis of Section~\ref{sec:exp1}} using the quantitative \textit{ephemerality} criterion below; for the experimental subset we additionally require that the word fill a recurring informal semantic slot (e.g.\ ``impressive/cool''), keeping meaning roughly controlled across eras.

\paragraph{Quantitative ephemerality criterion.}
We define ``ephemerality'' loosely and admit three trajectory shapes: words rising in popularity and peaking near the end of the dataset, words declining from an early peak, and words peaking somewhere in between. Every candidate must pass three criteria: (1) at least 80 slang-sense hits across 2013--2024, to exclude low-frequency words whose yearly rates are mostly sampling noise; (2) a minimum year at or below 50\% of the word's own peak year, i.e.\ usage changes by at least half within the dataset; and (3) at most 80\% of the word's raw occurrences rejected by the RoBERTa sense classifier at the 0.99 threshold, to exclude highly polysemous words (e.g.\ \textit{goals}, \textit{extra}, \textit{tight}) whose occurrences are overwhelmingly a standard-English sense.

Applying these criteria to the 144 candidates yields 40 ``ephemeral'' target words; the polysemy criterion is the most restrictive, removing 42 of the 82 words that pass the first two. The full evaluation across all 144 words can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:ephemerality}. The target words for subsequent experiments, grouped by peak year, are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pre-2018 (peaked 2013--2016, declining since):} \textit{dude, hella, lmfao, omg, stfu, finna, lol, meh, lmao, thot, griefing, rad, yapping, bro, methinks, troll, u, ick, pumped}.
  \item \textbf{Around 2020 (peaked 2019--2021):} \textit{cucked, shitposting, struggle bus, bruh, simp}.
  \item \textbf{2022--2024 (most recent):} \textit{lowkey, shitpost, \texttt{U+1F480} (skull emoji), doomscrolling, glow-up, hooman, vibing, catfished, idgaf, legit, situationship, vibes, yeet, cuck, red pill, doggo}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Experiment 1: Corpus Frequency Over Time}
\label{sec:exp1}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:exp1-method}

\textbf{Goal.} Establish a reference temporal frequency distribution of target slang words across time-sliced web data, serving as a ground truth against which LLM output distributions can be compared.

\textbf{Data source.} We use the \textbf{FineWeb} \citep{penedo2024the} 10BT sample hosted on HuggingFace, which carries a \texttt{dump} field tagging each document with its originating CC-MAIN crawl (year-week), allowing us to slice the sample by crawl date\footnote{Crawl date's accuracy as a proxy for our true desired metric \textit{published date} is reliant on the date sources effective deduplication, i.e. how well any given dump does excluding old material not introduced since the last checkpoint.}. FineWeb is itself a filtered, deduplicated derivative of Common Crawl, so it both reflects the kind of corpus used to train modern LLMs and removes much of the boilerplate and duplication that makes raw Common Crawl analysis difficult and unreliable. FineWeb processing provides data spanning 2013--2024, which matches with the published cutoff date for our open models used.

\textbf{Sense disambiguation.} A central difficulty is that many target words are polysemous: \textit{sick} (ill vs.\ excellent), \textit{troll} (creature vs.\ online provocateur), \textit{aura} (energy field vs.\ Gen-Z coolness). To address disambiguating the slang and non-slang senses during counting, we use a methodology inspried by \citep{aloraini2026slangdetection}, we extract the textual context of every target-word occurrence and score it with a \textbf{RoBERTa classifier fine-tuned to distinguish the slang sense} from the standard sense. Only occurrences scoring above a threshold ($\geq 0.99$, selected to minimize false positives) are counted as slang usage. See Appendix \ref{app:classifier} for full details of our evaluation of this schema.

\textbf{Normalization.} Sense-filtered counts are converted to a rate expressed as occurrences per million tokens of the corresponding dump:
\[
r_{w,d} = \frac{\text{count}_{\text{slang}}(w,\, d)}{\text{tokens}(d)} \times 10^{6}
\]
where \textit{w} is a target word and \textit{d} is a crawl dump. Per-dump token totals are taken from the FineWeb sample metadata. This normalization controls for the fact that successive dumps vary substantially in size.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} The 10BT sample is a random draw from FineWeb, so a per-dump occurrence count $k$ is modeled as $k \sim \text{Poisson}(r_{w,d}\cdot \text{tokens}(d))$. We report the exact Poisson interval (95\% by default) as a shaded band around each word's rate curve; bands are wide for rare words and tight for common ones.

\textbf{Output.} For each target word we plot $r_{w,d}$ against dump date, producing the temporal frequency curves in Section~\ref{sec:exp1}. These curves are the corpus ground truth against which the LLM generated word distributions are compared.

Slicing the FineWeb 10BT sample by crawl dump and applying the RoBERTa sense filter yields, for each target word, a curve of slang-sense occurrences per million tokens against dump date (2013--2024). The band figures below plot each curve as a percentage of that word's own peak dump, so words spanning two orders of magnitude in absolute rate can be compared by shape on a shared axis; the absolute peak rates are reported in Table~\ref{tab:words}. These curves confirm that the target words exhibit the ephemeral lifecycles the querying experiments rely on, and they separate cleanly into the three temporal bands of Section~\ref{sec:exp1-method}, which we present in Figure~\ref{fig:fineweb-trends}.

Two properties matter for the following response vocabulary analysis. First, the words we treat as ``older'' (\textit{lol}, \textit{swag}) are demonstrably in decline by the end of the window, while the ``recent'' words (\textit{aura}, \textit{slay}, \textit{glow-up}) are still rising. A true-to-life model of slang usage would emphasize using more recent and relevant slang words, while deemphasizing older slang that has become dated and much less popular. Second, the sense filter is what makes these counts meaningful: without it, \textit{sick} and \textit{aura} would be dominated by their standard-English senses and show no slang lifecycle at all. Additional details regarding the sense filtering process, such as model recall and precision, are included in the appendix.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Direct Year Association}
\label{sec:exp2}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:exp2-method}

\textbf{Goal:} Experiment~3 measures \textit{usage}, essentially what slang the model uses when it is not told to think about time. Experiment~2 instead measures \textit{meta-knowledge}: when asked directly, does the model know when a slang word was popular? Contrasting the two isolates whether failures are a knowledge gap or a usage bias. This is the inverse of the Experiment~3 design constraint: here we deliberately provide a temporal cue via the prompt.

We use three prompt forms, each rendered once per target word and sampled across all four models:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{GiveYear:} \textit{``The slang word `\{word\}' is most associated with what year? Respond using only the year.''}: directly requesting the word's peak year.
  \item \textbf{DatePhrase:} \textit{``You hear someone saying `\{word\}' in conversation, which year are they most likely saying this in?''}: a softer, scenario-framed variant of the same question.
  \item \textbf{EraAppropriate:} \textit{``It's \{year\}, is it appropriate to use the term `\{word\}'?''}: a year-conditioned appropriateness judgement swept over 2013--2025. Rather than asking the model to \emph{name} a year, this form fixes the year and asks the model to \emph{judge} whether the term fits it, testing whether meta-knowledge of slang timing surfaces as a usage judgement.
\end{enumerate}

GiveYear and DatePhrase are sampled five times per word per model, and we parse a four-digit year from each free-text response; comparing the model's stated peak year against the corpus peak from Experiment~1 tells us whether a model's \textit{declarative} knowledge of slang timing is accurate. EraAppropriate is sampled five times per (word, year) pair and each answer is classified yes/no --- if a model tracked usage, appropriateness should fall off after a word's corpus peak. The full prompt set (including supplementary logprob and inverse-mapping prompts) and per-prompt counts are given in Appendix~\ref{app:exp2}.

\subsubsection{Findings}

Experiment~2 asks what the models \textit{know} about slang timing, by posing the temporal question directly. We see that while models carry \emph{directionally} correct declarative knowledge of when slang was popular, that knowledge is both noticeably shifted toward the past and not reflected in their spontaneous usage (Experiment~3).

\textbf{The figure overlays model belief against corpus ground truth.} Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc} places the target words on the y-axis \emph{ordered by their true corpus peak year} (from Experiment~1), with calendar year on the x-axis. Because the words are sorted by true peak, the stars form a rising staircase, so the horizontal offset of each diamond from its star reads directly as how far the model is off.

\textbf{Models order slang roughly by era but compress it toward the past.} The diamonds broadly follow the staircase --- models do place \textit{lol}, \textit{troll}, and \textit{bro} early and \textit{aura} and \textit{slay} late --- but they systematically date the newer terms too early. For \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, \textit{alpha} (corpus peak 2020) is placed near 2010, and \textit{vibe}/\textit{vibes} (still rising through 2024) near 2018/2020. The mean absolute error against the corpus peak is sizeable for every model: 6.5 years for Claude, 7.1 for Llama, and 10.3 for DeepSeek, consistent with a sense of slang anchored to an earlier window than current usage.

\textbf{Caveat on the comparison.} The corpus ``peak'' is the year of maximum frequency, so for the words still rising at the end of the data window (2023--2024 peaks) it is closer to a lower bound on recency; a model dating \textit{vibe} to 2018 is arguably reporting the \emph{onset} of popular use rather than the corpus peak. The comparison is cleanest for words that have visibly crested within the window (\textit{lol}, \textit{swag}, \textit{alpha}), where the models still skew early. \texttt{gpt-4o} is a special case: its lower 5.5-year error is an artifact rather than better calibration, since its \textit{DatePhrase} responses collapse to ``2023'' (close to its cutoff date) for almost every word, which happens to sit near the many recent peaks.

\textbf{Asked to judge rather than name a year, models never call slang dated.} A complementary query set (\textit{EraAppropriate}, Appendix~\ref{app:exp2-era}) fixes the year in the prompt and asks whether a term is appropriate to use then. If the directional timing knowledge in Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc} were carried through to a usage judgement, appropriateness would fall off after a word's corpus peak. It does not: across 2013--2025 the models answer ``appropriate'' for almost every (word, year) pair (yes-rate 100\% for \texttt{gpt-4o}, 96.7\% for Claude, 88.4\% for DeepSeek, 78.6\% for Llama), and for no word does the yes-rate decline after the peak except under \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B}, the only model showing even a partial drop-off ($84\%\rightarrow72\%$). Even the era knowledge the models demonstrably have thus fails to surface when the same fact is queried as an appropriateness judgement.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The models possess \emph{directional} temporal knowledge but that knowledge is both compressed toward the past and, critically, not carried through in their actual usage (Experiment~3). The failure to produce current slang is therefore not a total absence of temporal knowledge, but shows the divergence between model's declarative knowledge and behavior, a particularly important discrepancy for evaluting how models may interact with language trends.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Semantic Slot Querying}
\label{sec:exp3}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:exp3-method}

\textbf{Goal:} Measure what slang a model actually \textit{produces} when asked for an informal word, with no temporal cue in the prompt, so we observe natural usage rather than meta-knowledge about eras. A model whose vocabulary tracked current usage should readily produce recent slang; a temporally frozen model should fall back on older, well-attested terms. A central design constraint is that we avoid naming years or providing informal language queues in the prompt, which would test meta-knowledge or register-matching instead of general usage.

We give the model a temporally neutral semantic description of a meaning and ask for a single informal word. The test runs in two modes:

\paragraph{Concept-slot mode.} The description names a general meaning with several era-spanning valid answers (``something impressive/cool'', ``mediocre'', ``charisma'', ``suspicious'', \dots) and we record which word the model reaches for, surfacing its default vocabulary per slot without naming any target word.

\paragraph{Target-recovery mode.} The description is written to point at one specific target word from Section~\ref{sec:exp1-method} --- e.g.\ a definition that uniquely evokes \textit{gaslight} or \textit{aura} --- and we measure how often the model returns that exact word, counting stem and inflection matches (so \textit{glow-up}/\textit{glowup} and \textit{gaslight}/\textit{gaslighting} all count). Recovery rate is a direct measure of how reluctant the model is to produce the intended term, and of whether the term is effectively out of vocabulary. Each target word is queried for with three independently worded phrasings to control for wording, and each phrasing is sampled ten times per model.

\paragraph{Year-conditioned variant.} To test whether a model conditions its vocabulary on a stated time, we prepend ``The year is \{year\}.'' to each target-recovery prompt and sweep \{year\} over 2013--2025. If a model were aware of its own data distribution, we would expect recovery accuracy for a word should be highest at, or near, the year of that word's true corpus peak (Experiment~1). Flat accuracy across years would indicate the model ignores the temporal cue; a peak at the wrong year would indicate a miscalibrated sense of timing, or one based on something other than dataset distribution.

\subsubsection{Findings}

This experiment is our first framework for querying models to explore their awareness of slang's temporal characteristics. We generate a selection of prompts that are intended to solicit one of a set of target words from the models, and test their ability to predict the intended word. The list of target words is the set of slang words from section one, with known temporal characteristics. We run two variations of this experiment: One with a free response from the model from which target words are identified, and a second where the model chooses from a multiple choice set with the target slang term as one of the options.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Persona Prompting}
\label{sec:exp4}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:exp4-method}

\textbf{Goal:} The other queries all pin the model down to a single-word answer. Experiment 4 attempts to invert the goal of querying information about known slang words from the models, instead seeking to find what slang is commonly generated by models and then analyzing the characteristics of that set of words.

\textbf{Prompts.} We give the model a persona instead of a question. The system prompt tells it to act like ``a young person using informal language like you would among peers,'' and two user prompts ask it to simulate a short text-message or group-chat exchange between friends. The prompt never names a target word, a year, or even the word ``slang,'' so whatever informal vocabulary shows up is volunteered rather than elicited. We collect 100 free-form responses per model (400 in total) at temperature~1.0.

\textbf{Scoring via effective vintage.} To turn free text into a temporal measurement, we scan each response for words from a 150-item slang lexicon (generated by combining the words from section 1 with manually extracted phrases/variants from this experiment's responeses) and tag every hit with that word's corpus peak year \footnote{Peak year is calculated using the word counts generated in section 1. For words added to the set from the experiment 4 responses we re-ran an identical set of steps to initial slang collection from section 1 to find words counts per dump.}. We then summarise a model's output by its \textit{effective vintage}: the occurrence-weighted mean peak year of the slang it produced. A model's datedness can be measured by how far behind present day it's \textit{effective vintage} lies. As a baseline we compute the same weighted mean over the lexicon's corpus frequencies (its \textit{corpus vintage}), so the reference point is the corpus's own centre of mass rather than the calendar, since both are expected to show some offset from present day. This frame of reference serves to confirm that the model does not exhibit emergent behavior that might bring it's slang usage closer to current trends. Appendix~\ref{app:usersim} gives the estimator, its bootstrap confidence intervals, and a distributional cross-check.

\subsubsection{Findings}

Experiment 4 inverts the direction of investigation, starting with relatively free form "persona" generations by the models, and analysing the generated vocabularies temporal properties. Figure~\ref{fig:usersim-slang} counts how often each lexicon word appears across the 400 persona responses, ordered by corpus peak year (top panel) and by corpus frequency (bottom). A few long-lived, high-frequency terms dominate: \textit{u}, \textit{lol}, and \textit{omg} together make up roughly 79\% of all in-lexicon slang, and adding \textit{bro} brings the four terms that peaked in 2013--2014 to about 84\% of the total. Recent slang, such as \textit{vibe}, \textit{slay}, \textit{legit}, \textit{aura}, turn up in the low single digits or not at all, echoing the recovery gap from the semantic-slot queries.

\textbf{The output carries an early-2010s vintage.} Putting a number on the skew, the slang these models emit has an occurrence-weighted effective vintage of \textbf{2014.0} (95\% CI $[2013.9, 2014.1]$; Poisson bootstrap), roughly \textbf{1.7 years older} than the \textbf{2015.8} vintage of the same lexicon in the corpus (Figure~\ref{fig:cc-aggregate}). Every model skews old, so this is not a single outlier dragging the average down. We do not observe any emergent bias in the models to generate more recent slang words to better fit with trends in language. Instead we unsurprisingly see a relatively close match to the distribution in the training data, with a tendency to overuse phrases that show recent decreases in popularity (lol, omg).

\textbf{Reluctance versus recency.} The models differ mainly in how much slang they volunteer at all: \texttt{gpt-4o} produces the fewest in-lexicon hits of the four (205, against 881 for \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}), in keeping with its general reluctance to slip into a casual register unprompted \cite{}. What none of the four do is reach for current slang; the early-2010s skew holds across all of them.

\section{Results}

Findings from the corpus analysis and the query experiments converge on a consistent pattern:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The target words have genuine, separable lifecycles.} The corpus analysis (Experiment~1, Figure~\ref{fig:fineweb-trends}) confirm that the older terms (\textit{lol}, \textit{swag}) are in decline by the end of the window while the newest terms (\textit{aura}, \textit{slay}, \textit{glow-up}) are still rising. This is what makes the words usable as a temporal probe and informs the curated target set selection.

  \item \textbf{Models are least fluent in the most current slang.} In semantic-slot recovery (Experiment~3, Figure~\ref{fig:slotaccuracy}), every model recovers older, unambiguous terms (\textit{lol}, \textit{troll}, \textit{gaslight}, \textit{glow-up}) near-perfectly, but recovery collapses for the newest term \textit{aura} and for polysemous slots (\textit{sick}, \textit{swag}) where the model prefers a competing or literal word. The slang that is most current in human usage is the slang the models are least able to produce on demand.

  \item \textbf{An explicit year cue does not fix it.} Prefixing the recovery prompt with ``The year is \{year\}.'' (Experiment~3, Figure~\ref{fig:slotyear}) barely changes which slang the model produces: most words are recovered at a flat rate across 2013--2025, and the words that do vary do not peak at their true corpus-peak year --- for the most recent terms (\textit{red pill}, \textit{vibe}) accuracy is, if anything, highest when an \emph{earlier} year is named. The stated date does not seem to influence the models' confidence in choosing out-of-date slang terms.

  \item \textbf{Parametric knowledge vs usage are unaligned.} The year-association queries (Experiment~2, Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc}) show that models order slang by era directionally correctly, but place the newer terms years too early relative to the corpus peak (mean absolute error 5.5--10.3 years per model), and their spontaneous usage does not track recency at all. The temporal failures contain both a usage bias and a backward-shifted sense of slang timing.

  \item \textbf{Freeform usage patterns reflect bias against modern slang.} When the models are prompted to write casually in a persona (Experiment~4, Figure~\ref{fig:usersim-slang}), the slang they volunteer still skews old. Occurrence-weighted effective vintage (as outlined in Experiment 4's methodology section) is 2014.0, about 1.7 years behind the 2015.8 vintage of the same lexicon in the corpus, and a handful of long-lived terms (\textit{u}, \textit{lol}, \textit{omg}, \textit{bro}) account for the bulk of usage.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}

Results suggest the models are not sensitive to temporal trends in their spontaneous slang usage. When asked to produce current slang on demand they reliably return older, well-attested terms and struggle most with the newest ones (Experiment~3). An explicit year cue does not move them toward era-appropriate vocabulary, even while they retain \emph{directionally} correct declarative knowledge of when those terms were popular (Experiment~2). This is consistent with the hypothesis that model slang \textit{usage} reflects total frequency in the training corpus rather than a temporally calibrated view of what is current. While this may be an intuitive result, we argue that the implications of this quality of LLMs has severe downstream implications for the model ecosystem and its societal impacts. \todo{Strengthen the framing with linguistics citations on the social and identity-signaling function of slang, motivating why temporally miscalibrated informal vocabulary matters independently of the downstream training-data argument.}

The difficulty models have producing the most recent term \textit{aura} (which is still sharply rising in the corpus at the end of the window in mid 2024) is striking precisely because the same models date it to roughly the right era (2023--2024) when asked directly (Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc}). \textit{aura} is the cleanest illustration of usage-bias-without-a-knowledge-gap: the model knows when the word belongs yet underrepesents it in generation. For most other recent terms the model additionally shifts its stated date years too early, so there is unclear declarative knowledge in addition to miscalibrated usage in generation. The reliability gap between recovering older versus newer slang may reflect that the older terms have saturated the training data with strong, redundant signal, whereas the newest terms appear in quantities too small (and too close to the data-collection horizon) to anchor confident generation, despite being the dominant form in current human usage.

\subsection{Implications for Training Data}

If models cannot keep pace with linguistic change, the effect compounds as web text becomes increasingly model-generated \citep{shumailov2023curse}: output reflecting stagnant, older vocabulary re-enters future training corpora, further entrenching dated language and eroding models' capacity to represent current human usage. This connects to the ``stochastic parrots'' critique \citep{bender2021parrots}---models capture the statistical structure of language but need not track its dynamics, leading to a fundamentally inaccurate view of human language.

The practical effect today is modest, and is partly masked by instruction-tuned models' general reluctance to produce slang at all (Section~\ref{sec:exp3}) (which we would argue has further social implications as young people get a higher and higher percentage of their linguistic contact from models). The longer-term concern is both larger and more general than slang: that models lagging ability to reflect current usage trends makes them a perpetually outdated interface, and inaccurate representations of language (or by extension personas). As models are retrained on the accumulated text of past decades---increasingly including their own outputs---the language they produce may drift steadily further from contemporary usage. Mitigating this will require explicit training interventions, potentially things like recency-weighted sampling, together with a sustained supply of fresh, verifiably human-authored text. Our results add weight to the case for treating human-generated text as a premium training resource critical to maintaining models that use language accurately.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Cross-model comparisons confound knowledge cutoff with architecture, training data, and alignment procedure (e.g.\ RLHF); we therefore emphasize within-model temporal patterns over cross-model magnitudes.
  \item All four models experimented on are instruction-tuned. RLHF and instruction tuning are known to suppress non-standard English \citep{barnhart2025rlhf} and may amplify the apparent temporal freeze. Replicating on base models would isolate this effect, but would limit us to experimenting on open models instead of more popular closed ones.
  \item Quality filtering and deduplication in Common Crawl derivatives can distort raw term frequencies; our FineWeb-based rates inherit any such distortion, and the de-facto upsampling of older dumps \citep{cheng2024dated} biases the corpus baseline toward earlier usage. Mitigation of this is sought by the FineWeb filtering methodologies. Additionally, the conclusions here are concerned with the pragmatic effects of the training data on model language, so distortions in training data do not detract from the conclusions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Work}

\paragraph{Mitigation Suggested by Related Work} From related work around staleness of models with regard to factual information there are several suggested mitigations that could apply here. \citet{zhang2023mitigatingtemporal} model how long a fact remains true and discard facts whose predicted duration has expired, trading recall for reduced staleness in open-domain QA. \citet{zhao2024temporal} show that a pretrained model's internal sense of ``now'' lags its actual data, and that this internal clock can be \textit{reset} by conditioning on the current year in the prompt (which we demonstrate does not hold for the case of slang vocabulary correction), or by lightweight finetuning --- recovering a substantial share of the lost performance. Exploring how finetuning might improve vocabulary used by a model is not explored in this paper and would be an interesting future set of experiments.
Additionally, we suggest that current RLHF also has a strong effect on language style used by models \todo{citations}.
Correctly using RLHF from annotators that are familiar with the correct use of modern langauge could also improve the ability of models to correctly prefer up-to-date language.

\paragraph{Further directions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The effects of RLHF are suspected to be a strong factor is current model vocabularies. Exploring these effects could involve replicating the queries on base (non-instruction-tuned) checkpoints to isolate the contribution of RLHF to slang suppression.
  \item In coming years there will be a longer and longer history of models to study their evolution over time. To emulate a version of this we could run a longitudinal study tracking vocabulary drift as models are trained on successive crawl snapshots, which is unfortunately outside of the resource scope of this project (as it requires repeated, expensive pretraining).
  \item Multilingual analysis: low-resource languages may be affected more strongly (model output can quickly dominate scarce human data) or less (smaller corpora may be more easily refreshed by recent text).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Word Selection Criteria and Statistics}
\label{app:ephemerality}

Each of the 144 candidate words is scored on three statistics, and a word joins
the target set only if it passes all three:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Frequency floor.} At least 80 slang-sense hits (occurrences scoring
  $\geq 0.99$ on the sense classifier) summed over 2013--2024. Below this,
  percent-of-peak values are dominated by sampling noise: a word with a handful of
  hits can read 0\% in several years purely by chance.
\item \textbf{Ephemerality.} The word's lowest year must sit at or below 50\% of
  its own peak year, i.e.\ usage changes by at least half between peak and trough
  within the dataset. Rising, falling, and peaked trajectories all qualify.
\item \textbf{Polysemy.} At most 80\% of the word's raw occurrences may be rejected
  by the RoBERTa sense classifier at the 0.99 threshold. A word whose occurrences
  are overwhelmingly a standard-English sense (e.g.\ \textit{goals}, \textit{extra},
  \textit{tight}) is too ambiguous to be read as slang by a model without context,
  so it is excluded even if its slang sense is genuinely ephemeral.
\end{enumerate}

Of the 144 candidates, 45 fail the frequency floor, 17 fail the ephemerality
criterion, and 66 fail the polysemy criterion (many fail more than one), leaving
40 target words (Figure~\ref{fig:ephemerality_initial_filter}). The polysemy
criterion does most of the work: the first two criteria alone pass 82 words, and
the third removes 42 of them. Table~\ref{tab:selection} lists every candidate with
its three statistics. The thresholds are set as constants in
\texttt{FineWebAnalysis/plot\_min\_pct.py}; filter rates are read from
\texttt{word\_filter\_rates\_99.csv}.

\section{Experiment 1: FineWeb Corpus Frequency}
\label{app:exp1}

\subsection{Corpus and token budget}

The corpus ground truth is the public FineWeb \citep{penedo2024the} \texttt{sample-10BT} config on HuggingFace: 95 dated Common Crawl (\texttt{CC-MAIN}) dumps spanning \texttt{2013-20} through \texttt{2024-10}, totalling $1.037\times10^{10}$ tokens. Each document's \texttt{dump} field is mapped to the Monday of its ISO year-week to place it on a calendar axis, and the per-dump token totals (cached in \texttt{fineweb\_10BT\_dump\_sizes.json}) serve as the denominators for rate normalisation. The sample is unevenly distributed across years---the mid-2010s crawls are the largest and the truncated 2024 slice the smallest---so per-year token totals (Table~\ref{tab:tokens}) are essential for interpreting raw counts.

\subsection{Context extraction}

For each target word we stream the sample and, for every surviving document, extract a $\pm 20$-token window of the original text around each match (\texttt{fineweb\_context.py}, \texttt{--context-window 20}), preserving the raw casing, punctuation, and spacing needed for sense classification. Matching is performed on normalised tokens (whitespace-split, lower-cased, leading/trailing punctuation stripped) via a regex that mirrors the tokeniser's word boundaries, so multi-word targets (\textit{red pill}, \textit{glow-up}) and inflected forms are captured. One CSV of \texttt{(target, uri, target\_context)} rows is produced per dump.

\subsection{Sense disambiguation}

Each extracted context is scored with a RoBERTa classifier fine-tuned to separate the \textit{slang} sense from the \textit{standard-English} sense (e.g.\ \textit{sick} ill vs.\ excellent, \textit{aura} energy-field vs.\ Gen-Z coolness). The judged word is injected into a prompt so the decision is conditioned on \textit{which} word is in question: \texttt{Is the word "\{target\}" used as slang here? \{context\}}. The paper's figures count only occurrences scoring $\geq 0.99$; the threshold is a tunable parameter exposed by \texttt{roberta\_filter.py} (raw scores lie in $[-1,1]$). Appendix~\ref{app:classifier} documents the classifier's data and evaluation.

\subsection{Normalisation and uncertainty}

Sense-filtered counts are converted to occurrences per million tokens of the corresponding dump, $r_{w,d} = 10^{6}\cdot \text{count}_{\text{slang}}(w,d) / \text{tokens}(d)$, using the per-dump token totals above. Each per-dump count is modelled as a Poisson draw $k \sim \text{Poisson}(r_{w,d}\cdot\text{tokens}(d))$, and the plotter can shade the exact (Garwood) Poisson interval (95\% by default) as a band that tightens as the centred smoothing window aggregates more of the sample. The band figures in this paper are drawn without that shading (\texttt{--no-ci}), which at 95 dumps and up to eight overlapping words obscured the lines it annotated.

\subsection{Plotting and peak extraction}

The band figures (Figure~\ref{fig:fineweb-trends}) use a linear y-axis rescaled per word to a percentage of that word's peak smoothed dump (the \texttt{\_pct} variant, \texttt{--percent-of-peak}) with a 9-dump centred moving average and no shaded CI band. The rescaling removes the need for the broken y-axis the earlier \texttt{\_count} figures used to stop \textit{lol} compressing the other curves. Dumps with zero sense-filtered occurrences are dropped so the line connects neighbouring dumps rather than falling to the floor, and a $\log_2$-axis version of each figure is also produced. The corpus peak year (Table~\ref{tab:words}) is the argmax, over calendar years, of the year-pooled normalised rate (\texttt{peak\_year.py}). All band figures are generated by \texttt{word\_rate\_plotter.py --threshold 0.99 --highlight-bands --percent-of-peak --no-ci --clean --settings-note --smooth 9}, which pools the target and scenario scored contexts.

\subsection{Implementation}

\texttt{FineWebAnalysis/}: \texttt{fineweb\_context.py} (context extraction), \texttt{roberta\_filter.py} (context scoring), \texttt{word\_rate\_plotter.py} (frequency curves and Poisson bands), \texttt{peak\_year.py} (peak-year extraction).

\section{Sense Classifier: Data, Training, and Validation}
\label{app:classifier}

\subsection{Model and training}

The sense classifier is a \texttt{roberta-base} sequence classifier fine-tuned for binary slang-vs-standard judgement (\texttt{finetune\_roberta.py}). The target word is injected into the prompt template above and the rendered string is truncated/padded to 256 tokens. Training uses a class-weighted cross-entropy loss (weights inversely proportional to class frequency, to offset the label imbalance), AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, weight decay $0.01$), batch size 16, and a linear schedule; we hold out a stratified 20\% split (\texttt{random\_state=42}) and keep the checkpoint with the best validation accuracy. The exact prompt template is serialised next to the model so that \texttt{roberta\_filter.py} applies identical wording at inference.

\subsection{Annotation data}

Training data combines human-annotated FineWeb contexts (1{,}310 rows over 15 words, 649 slang / 661 standard) with a larger synthetically generated set (8{,}504 rows over 124 words (104 unique slang + 21 common forms, e.g. yap, yapping or rage bait, rage-bait), 6{,}108 slang / 2{,}396 standard). Synthetic examples are produced by prompting Claude Opus 4.7 for 50--100 natural example sentences per target word, each self-labelled slang or standard; words with a genuine everyday sense are balanced $\sim$50/50, while slang-only words (\textit{skibidi}, \textit{rizz}, \dots) are all-slang (\texttt{generate\_synthetic\_annotations.py}). Conditioning the classifier on the target word lets a single model generalise across the full target vocabulary rather than requiring a per-word classifier.
This set is manually validated by sampling. Out of 8504 text rows generated, we randomly sampled 1000* and manually validated Claude’s annotations.
Human annotator (me) agreed in 98.69\% of cases, and marked 1 (0.38\%) as false positives for slang and 12 (1.64\%) as false negatives for slang usage of the target word.


\subsection{Validation of the synthetic labels}

Because the training set leans on synthetic labels, we audited their reliability against human judgement on a 995-example sample (\texttt{validation\_metrics.py}), treating the manual annotation as ground truth. Synthetic labels agree with human labels 98.69\% of the time (982/995 samples). Other metrics: precision 99.86\%, recall 98.36\%, $F_1 = 99.11\%$; false-positive rate 0.38\%, false-negative rate 1.64\%; confusion matrix TN\,=\,261, FP\,=\,1, FN\,=\,12, TP\,=\,721, confirming the synthetic labels are a faithful stand-in for human sense judgements.

\subsection{Held-out evaluation protocol}

The trained classifier is evaluated on a separate, class-balanced held-out set of FineWeb contexts (1{,}340 rows over 15 words, 670 slang / 670 standard; \texttt{fine\_web\_small\_validation\_set}) via \texttt{auc\_eval.py}, which reports ROC AUC together with a confusion matrix, precision/recall/$F_1$ at the operating threshold, and a per-word breakdown. Passing \texttt{--train-dir} additionally restricts the metrics to contexts whose text does not appear in the training annotations, isolating performance on genuinely unseen strings. On this held-out set, the final \texttt{ft\_model\_roberta} checkpoint attains an overall ROC AUC of \textbf{0.9906}; at the paper's operating threshold ($\geq 0.99$, i.e.\ \texttt{ft\_score} $\geq 0.98$) precision is \textbf{94.44\%} and recall is \textbf{96.42\%} ($F_1 = 95.42\%$; confusion matrix TN\,=\,632, FP\,=\,38, FN\,=\,24, TP\,=\,646). Restricting to the 1{,}234 rows (92.1\%) whose context does not overlap the training annotations (\texttt{completed\_annotations}) via \texttt{--train-dir} gives an unseen-only ROC AUC of \textbf{0.9924}, precision \textbf{94.48\%}, and recall \textbf{96.77\%} ($F_1 = 95.61\%$), confirming the classifier's performance is not an artefact of train/validation leakage.

\section{Experiment 2: Direct Year Association}
\label{app:exp2}

These queries share the system message \texttt{"Give the response as a single returned token or word."} Two are free-text point estimates and the remainder are logprob/echo queries over the same nineteen target words and the year range 2013--2025:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{GiveYear:} ``The slang word `\{word\}' is most associated with what year? Respond using only the year.''
  \item \textbf{DatePhrase:} ``You hear someone saying `\{word\}' in conversation, which year are they most likely saying this in?''
  \item \textbf{GiveTopSlang:} ``What is the most unique and popular slang word associated with the year \{year\}? Respond using only a single word.'' --- the inverse mapping (year\,$\to$\,word), collected as free text asnd parsed; reported as supplementary.
  \item \textbf{EraAppropriate:} ``It's \{year\}, is it appropriate to use the term `\{word\}'?'' --- a year-conditioned appropriateness judgement swept over 2013--2025 for each target word. Unlike GiveYear/DatePhrase, which ask the model to \emph{name} a year, this experiment fixes the year in the prompt and asks the model to \emph{judge} whether the term fits it, testing whether the model treats a slang word as dated once it is past its era.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent GiveYear and DatePhrase are rendered once per target word and sampled five times per word on all four models ($19\times5\times2\times4 = 760$ responses); a four-digit year is parsed from each response. The mean responded year per word, pooled over GiveYear and DatePhrase, is plotted against the corpus peak in Figure~\ref{fig:yearassoc}; the per-model mean absolute errors against the corpus peak are 6.5 (Claude), 5.5 (GPT-4o, inflated by its collapse to ``2023''), 10.3 (DeepSeek), and 7.1 (Llama) years.

\subsection{EraAppropriate: year-conditioned appropriateness judgement}
\label{app:exp2-era}
The EraAppropriate prompt is swept over all nineteen target words and the thirteen prompt years 2013--2025, sampled five times per (word, year) pair on all four models ($19\times13\times5\times4 = 4{,}940$ responses). Each free-text answer is classified as \emph{yes} (appropriate), \emph{no} (inappropriate/dated), or \emph{unclear} by a lexical parser keyed on the leading yes/no token; the unclear rate is negligible ($\leq 0.4\%$ for every model). For each model we plot the fraction of decided responses answering ``yes'' as a heatmap of target word (y-axis, ordered by true corpus peak) against prompt year (x-axis), with each word's true corpus peak outlined (Figure~\ref{fig:eraappropriate}). A model that tracked usage would answer ``yes'' up to roughly a word's peak year and switch to ``no'' afterward.

Instead, the models judge slang to be perennially appropriate: the overall yes-rate is 100.0\% for \texttt{gpt-4o}, 96.7\% for \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, 88.4\% for \texttt{DeepSeek-V4-Pro}, and 78.6\% for \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B}. Critically, appropriateness does not fall off after a word's true peak. Splitting each word's cells into pre/at-peak versus post-peak years, the yes-rate is essentially unchanged for Claude ($96.4\%\rightarrow97.2\%$) and GPT-4o ($100\%\rightarrow100\%$) --- not one of the nineteen words shows a post-peak decline --- and it actually \emph{rises} for DeepSeek ($85.9\%\rightarrow91.8\%$). Only \texttt{Llama-3.3-70B} shows a modest dating effect ($84.0\%\rightarrow71.6\%$, with 7 of 19 words declining after their peak), consistent with its generally lower and noisier yes-rate. This is a third line of evidence, alongside the backward-shifted year associations above and the year-cue-insensitive recovery of Experiment~3, that the models lack an actionable sense of when a slang term is era-appropriate: they will neither stop reaching for dated slang nor, when asked directly, flag it as out of date.

  \item \textit{agreement:} (1)~``What's a single informal word someone uses to express strong enthusiastic agreement or approval?'' (2)~``Give me one slang word people say to mean `yes, absolutely, that's totally true.' '' (3)~``What's a casual word for when you hear something and you completely agree with it?'' (4)~``Name a single informal word used to affirm what someone just said --- like a stronger, more casual `exactly.' ''
  \item \textit{suspicious:} (1)~``What's a single informal word for when something about a person or situation feels off, untrustworthy, or not right?'' (2)~``Give me one slang word that means something seems off or like it shouldn't be trusted.'' (3)~``What's a casual word for when you sense that someone is being deceptive or hiding something?'' (4)~``Name a single informal word for a feeling that something is unsafe or that a person can't be trusted.''
  \item \textit{excellent (sensory):} (1)~``What's a single informal word someone would use to say a song, food, or piece of art is exceptionally good?'' (2)~``Give me one slang word people use when something sounds, tastes, or feels really, really good.'' (3)~``What's a casual word for when a song or meal is so good it gives you a visceral, satisfying reaction?'' (4)~``Name a single informal word meaning something is so good it almost overwhelms you in a great way.''
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Target-recovery mode.} Each of the nineteen target words has three phrasings written to evoke that word specifically. A response counts as a recovery if it matches the target after case-folding, applying a light suffix stemmer and whole-word phrase containment --- so \textit{glowup}/\textit{glow-up}, \textit{gaslighting}/\textit{gaslight}, and ``take the red pill''/\textit{red pill} all count, while \textit{swagger} is not credited as \textit{swag}. Listing~\ref{lst:exp3-recovery} shows two of the nineteen templates.

\textbf{Year-conditioned variant.} Each target-recovery prompt is prefixed with ``The year is \{year\}.'' and the year is swept over 2013--2025 (13 values). \section{Models and Sampling}
\label{app:models}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo} --- Together AI
  \item \texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro} --- Together AI
  \item \texttt{gpt-4o} --- OpenAI
  \item \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6} --- Anthropic
\end{enumerate}

\noindent A single harness (\texttt{PromptingSlang/}) dispatches each prompt to the correct provider by model-id convention (\texttt{org/model}\,$\to$\,Together, \texttt{claude-*}\,$\to$\,Anthropic, \texttt{gpt-*}/\texttt{o\#-*}\,$\to$\,OpenAI), retrying with exponential backoff. Prompts expand on their \texttt{variables} block, and each expansion is sampled \texttt{--samples} times; generation is capped at 512 tokens. All querying experiment prompts set temperature~1.0 per template. Because the closed APIs do not expose token logprobs, their response distributions are approximated by repeated sampling. Direct logprob/echo queries were intially run on the open models, but these were dropped in favor or consistency between the two model types. Runs are keyed by \texttt{(model, prompt, sample index)} and are resumable, so partial runs top-fill rather than restart. Prompt templates and the response-collection harness are in the \texttt{PromptingSlang/} directory of the project repository.

\section{Naturalistic Generation: Statistical Analyses}
\label{app:usersim}

This appendix details the persona-simulation queries of Experiment~4 (Section~\ref{sec:exp4}, Figure~\ref{fig:usersim-slang}) and the estimators that quantify its ``temporal vintage.''

\subsection{Prompt and vocabulary}

Each model is asked to role-play informal peer conversation via two prompts (system: \texttt{"You are a young person using informal language like you would among peers\dots"}; e.g.\ ``Simulate a brief conversation between two or more teenaged friends taking place online or over text message''), yielding 100 responses per model (400 total). Generated text is matched against a 150-word slang vocabulary (the union of \texttt{target\_words.txt} and \texttt{scenario\_words.txt}), each word carrying the corpus peak year of Experiment~1. As in the forced-choice queries, output concentrates on a handful of high-frequency, older terms: pooled across models the top items are the skull emoji (\texttt{U+1F480}), \textit{u}, \textit{lol}, \textit{omg}, and \textit{bro}, while post-2020 slang appears with counts near~1.

\subsection{Effective vintage}

We summarise each model's produced slang by its \textit{effective vintage}: the count-weighted mean corpus-peak year of the slang it emits (\texttt{effective\_vintage.py}). Over the vocabulary of 88 words with $\geq 100$ corpus hits (31 of which appear across the 400 pooled responses, 1{,}848 in-lexicon hits), the model vintage is \textbf{2014.0} (95\% CI $[2013.9, 2014.1]$; parametric Poisson bootstrap, $B=5000$, seed~42) against a frequency-weighted corpus vintage of \textbf{2015.8} --- a lag of $-1.7$ years, i.e.\ models skew older than the corpus baseline over the same vocabulary. The high-frequency skull emoji (\texttt{U+1F480}) is excluded from the vintage estimators, as its corpus rate is unreliable under FineWeb's text-processing.
